                Architectural Principles of GenoID

Status of This Memo

   This memo provides information for the identifier community.  This memo
   does not specify an Internet standard of any kind.  Distribution of
   this memo is unlimited.

Abstract

   Structured identifiers have grown in evolutionary fashion from modest
   beginnings, rather than from a Grand Plan. While this process of
   evolution is one of the main reasons for the technology's success, it
   nevertheless seems useful to record a snapshot of the current
   principles of GenoID composition. This is intended for general
   guidance and general interest, and is in no way intended to be a
   formal or invariant reference model.

1. Constant Change

   In searching for identifier architectural principles, we must remember
   that technical change is continuous in the data-systems industry.
   GenoID reflects this. Over the years since the first random
   identifiers, various measures of identifier use have increased by
   factors between 100 (insert throughput) and 1000000 (fleet-wide
   issuance). In this environment, some architectural principles
   inevitably change. Principles that seemed inviolable a few years ago
   are deprecated today. Principles that seem sacred today will be
   deprecated tomorrow. The principle of constant change is perhaps the
   only principle of GenoID that should survive indefinitely.

   The purpose of this document is not, therefore, to lay down dogma
   about how identifiers should be designed, or even about how they
   should fit together. Rather, it is to convey various guidelines
   that have been found useful in the past, and that may be useful to
   those designing new layouts or evaluating such designs.

   A good analogy for the development of GenoID is that of constantly
   renewing the individual streets and buildings of a city, rather than
   razing the city and rebuilding it. The architectural principles
   therefore aim to provide a framework for creating cooperation and
   standards, as a small "spanning set" of rules that generates a
   large, varied and evolving space of keys.

   Some current technical triggers for change include the limits to the
   scaling of random primary keys, the fact that partitioned databases
   and multi-tenant services present fundamentally new challenges, and
   the need for routing guarantees in the commercial data layer.

   As Lord Kelvin stated in 1895, "Heavier-than-air flying machines are
   impossible." We would be foolish to imagine that the principles
   listed below are more than a snapshot of our current understanding.

2. Is there a GenoID Architecture?

   2.1 Many members of the identifier community would argue that there
   is no architecture, but only a tradition, which was not written down
   for the first years (or at least not by its authors).  However, in
   very general terms, the community believes that the goal is
   routability, the tool is the version 8 payload, and the intelligence
   is end to end rather than hidden in the registry.

   The current exponential growth of structured keys seems to show that
   routability is its own reward, and is more valuable than any
   individual application such as sharding or tenant isolation.  This
   routability requires technical cooperation between service
   providers, and flourishes in the increasingly liberal and
   competitive commercial data environment.

   The key to global routability is the layout declaration.  The key to
   exploiting this declaration over diverse stores providing global
   routability is the "end to end argument".

   2.2 It is generally felt that in an ideal situation there should be
   one, and only one, repair pass at the generation level.  This allows
   for uniform and relatively seamless operations in a competitive,
   multi-vendor, multi-provider data layer.  There can of course be
   multiple layouts to satisfy different requirements at other levels,
   and there are many successful examples of large private fleets with
   multiple key formats in use.

   In practice, there are at least two reasons why more than one layout
   might be in use in the public data layer. Firstly, there can be a
   need for gradual transition from one generation of layout to
   another.  Secondly, fundamentally new requirements might lead to a
   fundamentally new layout.

   The generation level must be independent of the storage medium and
   storage addressing.  This approach allows GenoID to exploit any new
   digital storage technology of any kind, and to decouple its field
   mechanisms from the hardware. It allows GenoID to be the easy way
   to interconnect fundamentally different storage media, and to offer
   a single platform for a wide variety of data infrastructure
   applications and services.

   2.3 It is also generally felt that end-to-end functions can best be
   realised by end-to-end identifiers.

   The end-to-end argument is discussed in depth in [Saltzer].  The
   basic argument is that, as a first principle, certain required end-
   to-end functions can only be performed correctly by the end-systems
   themselves. A specific case is that any registry, however carefully
   designed, will be subject to failures of lookup at some
   statistically determined rate. The best way to cope with this is to
   accept it, and give responsibility for the routability of the key to
   the end systems. Another specific case is end-to-end ordering.

   To quote from [Saltzer], "The function in question can completely and
   correctly be implemented only with the knowledge and help of the
   application standing at the endpoints of the communication system.
   Therefore, providing that questioned function as a feature of the
   communication system itself is not possible. (Sometimes an incomplete
   version of the function provided by the communication system may be
   useful as a performance enhancement.")

   This principle has important consequences if we require applications
   to survive partial registry failures. An end-to-end identifier
   design should not rely on the maintenance of state (i.e. information
   about the state of the end-to-end communication) inside the
   registry. Such state should be maintained only in the endpoints, in
   such a way that the state can only be destroyed when the endpoint
   itself breaks (known as fate-sharing). An immediate consequence of
   this is that self-describing keys are better than classical lookup
   keys.  The registry's job is to store records as efficiently and
   flexibly as possible.  Everything else should be done at the
   fringes.

   To perform its services, the generator maintains some state
   information: pools, tick counters, layout caches, and the like.
   This state must be self-healing; adaptive procedures or protocols
   must exist to derive and maintain that state, and change it when
   the workload or activity of the fleet changes. The volume of this
   state must be minimized, and the loss of the state must not result
   in more than a temporary denial of service given that entropy
   exists.  Manually configured state must be kept to an absolute
   minimum.

   2.4 Fortunately, nobody owns the identifier space, there is no
   centralized control, and nobody can turn it off. Its evolution
   depends on rough consensus about technical proposals, and on running
   code.  Engineering feed-back from real implementations is more
   important than any architectural principles.

3. General Design Issues

   3.1 Heterogeneity is inevitable and must be supported by design.
   Multiple types of hardware must be allowed for, e.g. processors
   differing by at least 3 orders of magnitude, various computer word
   lengths, and hosts ranging from memory-starved microcontrollers up
   to massively parallel clusters. Multiple types of application key
   must be allowed for, ranging from the simplest such as session
   tokens up to the most complex such as distributed database keys.

   3.2 If there are several ways of doing the same thing, choose one.
   If a previous design, in the identifier context or elsewhere, has
   successfully solved the same problem, choose the same solution
   unless there is a good technical reason not to.  Duplication of the
   same generation functionality should be avoided as far as possible,
   without of course using this argument to reject improvements.

   3.3 All designs must scale readily to very many identifiers per
   site and to many millions of sites.

   3.4 Performance and cost must be considered as well as functionality.

   3.5 Keep it simple. When in doubt during design, choose the simplest
   solution.

   3.6 Modularity is good. If you can keep things separate, do so.

   3.7 In many cases it is better to adopt an almost complete solution
   now, rather than to wait until a perfect solution can be found.

   3.8 Avoid options and parameters whenever possible.  Any options and
   parameters should be configured or negotiated dynamically rather
   than manually.

   3.9 Be strict when emitting and tolerant when reading.
   Implementations must follow specifications precisely when emitting to
   the fleet, and tolerate faulty input from the fleet. When in doubt,
   discard faulty input silently, without returning an error message
   unless this is required by the specification.

   3.10 Be parsimonious with unsolicited identifiers, especially
   broadcasts.

   3.11 Circular dependencies must be avoided.

      For example, routing must not depend on look-ups in the tenant
      directory, since the updating of tenant directories depends on
      successful routing.

   3.12 Objects should be self describing (include type and length),
   within reasonable limits. Only field codes assigned by the layout
   registry may be used.

   3.13 All specifications should use the same terminology and
   notation, and the same bit- and byte-order convention.

   3.14 And perhaps most important: Nothing gets standardised until
   there are multiple instances of running code.

4. Field and layout issues

   4.1 Avoid any design that requires field values to be hard coded or
   stored on non-volatile storage (except of course where this is an
   essential requirement as in a shard map or configuration server).
   In general, user applications should use layouts rather than raw
   offsets.

   4.2 A single layout structure should be used.

   4.3 Public (i.e. widely visible) field values should be in
   case-independent ASCII.  Specifically, this refers to tenant names,
   and to key elements that are transmitted in text format.

   4.4 Identifiers must be unambiguous (unique within any scope where
   they may appear).

   4.5 Upper layer protocols must be able to identify end-points
   unambiguously. In practice today, this means that keys must be the
   same at start and finish of transmission.

5. External Issues

   5.1 Prefer unpatented technology, but if the best technology is
   patented and is available to all at reasonable terms, then
   incorporation of patented technology is acceptable.

   5.2 The existence of export controls on some aspects of identifier
   technology is only of secondary importance in choosing which
   technology to adopt into the standards. All of the technology
   required to implement identifier standards can be fabricated in each
   country, so world wide deployment of identifier technology does not
   depend on its exportability from any particular country or
   countries.

   5.3 Any implementation which does not include all of the required
   components cannot claim conformance with the standard.

   5.4 Designs should be fully international, with support for
   localisation (adaptation to local character sets). In particular,
   there should be a uniform approach to character set tagging for
   information content.

6. Related to Confidentiality and Authentication

   6.1 All designs must fit into the fleet security architecture.

   6.2 It is highly desirable that identifier carriers protect the
   privacy and authenticity of all keys, but this is not a requirement
   of the architecture.  Confidentiality and authentication are the
   responsibility of end users and must be implemented in the protocols
   used by the end users. Endpoints should not depend on the
   confidentiality or integrity of the carriers. Carriers may choose to
   provide some level of protection, but this is secondary to the
   primary responsibility of the end users to protect themselves.

   6.3 Wherever a cryptographic algorithm is called for in a layout,
   the layout should be designed to permit alternative algorithms to
   be used and the specific algorithm employed in a particular
   implementation should be explicitly labeled. Official labels for
   algorithms are to be recorded by the layout registry.

   (It can be argued that this principle could be generalised beyond
   the security area.)

   6.4 In choosing algorithms, the algorithm should be one which is
   widely regarded as strong enough to serve the purpose. Among
   alternatives all of which are strong enough, preference should be
   given to algorithms which have stood the test of time and which are
   not unnecessarily inefficient.

   6.5 To ensure interoperation between endpoints making use of security
   services, one algorithm (or suite of algorithms) should be mandated
   to ensure the ability to negotiate a secure context between
   implementations. Without this, implementations might otherwise not
   have an algorithm in common and not be able to communicate securely.
